Bleeding a radiator properly

Ten minutes of work, and the result tells you something about the system either way.

Cold at the top, or cold at the bottom?

Before you reach for the key, feel the radiator when the heating has been on for twenty minutes. Where it is cold tells you what is wrong, and only one of the two answers is air.

  • Cold at the top, warm at the bottom — air, which rises to the highest point. Bleeding will fix it.
  • Cold at the bottom, warm at the top — sludge, which settles. Bleeding will do nothing. The system needs flushing or the radiator removing and cleaning.
  • Cold all over while others are hot — usually a closed or seized valve, or a thermostatic head stuck shut.

What you need

A radiator bleed key, which costs very little from any hardware shop, a cloth, and a small container. A flat-blade screwdriver fits some modern valves, but the key is less likely to round off the square.

How to do it

  1. Turn the heating off and let the system cool. Bleeding a hot system risks scalding water, and the circulating pump can draw more air in while it runs.
  2. Start with the radiator on the lowest floor furthest from the boiler, and work towards the boiler, then upstairs. Air moves upward and towards the end of the circuit, so this order avoids pushing it into radiators you have already done.
  3. Hold the cloth under the bleed valve at the top corner. Turn the key anti-clockwise, no more than a quarter to half a turn. You should hear a hiss.
  4. Wait for the hiss to stop and the first dribble of water to appear, then close the valve. Do not open it further to speed things up.
  5. Repeat on each radiator in turn, then check the boiler pressure gauge.

Check the pressure afterwards

Letting air out of a sealed system drops its pressure. After bleeding several radiators, the gauge will often read lower than it did, and the boiler may refuse to fire if it has fallen far enough.

Top it back up to the figure in your manual — see what your boiler pressure gauge is telling you for how, and for what the number means.

When the air keeps coming back

Bleeding the same radiator every few weeks is not a maintenance routine. It means air is getting in, and there are three usual explanations.

  • A small leak somewhere is letting pressure out and drawing air in as the system cools.
  • An automatic air vent, often on the boiler or in the loft, has failed and is admitting air rather than releasing it.
  • Corrosion inside the system is generating hydrogen. This one smells faintly of rotten eggs when you bleed, and it means the inhibitor has been used up and the radiators are corroding from the inside.

All three are worth investigating rather than living with. The third in particular gets more expensive the longer it runs.

When to call someone

Call if bleeding produces nothing but the radiator stays cold, if the same radiator needs doing repeatedly, if the water that comes out is black or smells, or if a bleed valve will not seal once you have opened it.

The short version

  • Cold at the top means air; cold at the bottom means sludge
  • System off and cool before you start
  • Furthest from the boiler first, downstairs before upstairs
  • Check the pressure afterwards
