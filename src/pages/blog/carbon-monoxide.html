Carbon monoxide: alarms and symptoms

You cannot see it, smell it or taste it. An alarm is the only reliable warning you will get.

If an alarm sounds, or you suspect it now

  1. Open the windows and doors.
  2. Turn off the appliance if you can do it safely.
  3. Get everyone out of the building.
  4. Call the National Gas Emergency Service on 0800 111 999 — free, 24 hours.
  5. Do not go back in until you are told it is safe.

If anyone has symptoms, get medical help as well as reporting it. Call 999 if someone has collapsed, is confused or is struggling to breathe. Otherwise contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day, and say you suspect carbon monoxide — it changes what they test for, and the test is time-sensitive.

Where it comes from

Carbon monoxide is produced when a carbon fuel burns without enough oxygen to burn completely. Any fuel-burning appliance can produce it: gas boilers, cookers and fires, but equally oil boilers, solid-fuel stoves, open fires and portable heaters.

The usual causes are an appliance that has not been serviced, a blocked or damaged flue, or a room that has had its ventilation reduced — a blocked air brick, a sealed-up chimney, new windows fitted to a room with an open-flued appliance in it.

A yellow or orange flame where it should be crisp and blue, sooty staining around an appliance, pilot lights that keep going out, and more condensation than usual on the windows are all worth taking seriously. None of them is conclusive, and their absence proves nothing.

The symptoms, and what makes them easy to miss

Headache is the most common. Then dizziness, nausea, breathlessness, tiredness, stomach pain and confusion.

The difficulty is that this is also what a winter virus feels like, and the season in which heating appliances run hardest is the same season in which people expect to feel unwell. The patterns worth noticing are these:

  • Symptoms that improve when you leave the house and return when you go back in.
  • More than one person in the building feeling unwell at the same time — including pets, which are often affected first.
  • Symptoms that follow the heating being on rather than following a pattern of infection.

Higher concentrations cause loss of consciousness and can be fatal within minutes. Lower concentrations over long periods do damage that is easy to attribute to something else entirely.

Choosing and placing an alarm

Buy one that is marked as meeting BS EN 50291, which is the British and European standard for carbon monoxide alarms in domestic premises. Check for that mark rather than for a price or a brand. A smoke alarm does not detect carbon monoxide, and the two are not interchangeable.

Placement follows from the fact that carbon monoxide mixes with air rather than sinking or rising sharply:

  • In the same room as each fuel-burning appliance, roughly one to three metres away from it horizontally.
  • At about head height when seated or sleeping — on a wall is usually easier than on a shelf.
  • Not directly above an appliance, not in a cupboard, not next to a window or a door, and not anywhere it will get damp.
  • In rooms where people sleep, and in any room with an appliance that is used regularly.

Follow the manufacturer instructions where they differ from the above — they are specific to the unit. Test it with the test button at the interval the maker specifies, and replace the whole unit at its end-of-life date. The sensor degrades whether or not it has ever been triggered, so an old alarm that beeps on test is not necessarily still working.

If you rent

Carbon monoxide alarm requirements in rented property are set by regulations that differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and they have been tightened in recent years. Check the rules for your nation rather than assuming, and raise it with your landlord if no alarm is fitted.

Separately, your landlord must have every gas appliance and flue checked annually — see landlord gas safety records.

The thing that actually prevents it

An alarm tells you something has already gone wrong. An annual service is what stops it going wrong: the engineer checks that the appliance is burning completely, that the flue takes the products of combustion outside, and that the ventilation is adequate.

See annual servicing. Fit the alarm as well — the two are not alternatives.

Alarm sounding now?

Windows open, appliance off, everyone out, then call:

0800 111 999

Free, 24 hours. If anyone has symptoms, call 999 or NHS 111 as well.

The short version

  • No colour, no smell, no taste
  • An alarm marked BS EN 50291 is the only reliable warning
  • Symptoms that ease when you leave the house are the clue
  • An annual service is what prevents it
