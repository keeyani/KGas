What your boiler pressure gauge is telling you

The gauge is the most useful diagnostic on the front of a boiler, and the one most often misread.

What the number means

Most domestic heating systems are sealed. The water in the radiators is a closed loop, kept under pressure so that it circulates properly and so that it does not boil in the heat exchanger. The gauge reads that pressure in bar.

On a cold system, most manufacturers want somewhere around 1 to 1.5 bar. Running warm, it will read higher — the water expands as it heats, and that is normal. What matters is the cold reading and, more than that, whether the cold reading is stable from month to month.

Check the figure in your boiler manual rather than relying on the range above. Manufacturers differ, and some gauges have a green band marked on the dial that does the job for you.

Pressure too low

Below about 1 bar, many boilers will shut down rather than run. Some show a low-pressure fault code; others simply stop producing heat and leave you guessing.

A sealed system should not lose pressure. If it does, the water is going somewhere, and there are only a few places it can go:

  • A small leak on a radiator valve, a joint or a towel rail — often slow enough to evaporate before it marks anything.
  • A leak on buried pipework, which may show as a warm patch on a floor rather than visible water.
  • A failed expansion vessel, which no longer absorbs the expansion and pushes water out through the pressure relief valve instead.
  • A weeping pressure relief valve, which often shows as a drip from a small pipe outside the house.
  • Air being released after bleeding radiators, which is a one-off rather than a pattern.

Topping up, and when to stop

Topping up is a reasonable thing to do once. Use the filling loop — usually a braided hose or a keyed valve under the boiler — and open it slowly until the gauge reads what the manual asks for. Close it fully afterwards and, if it is a loose hose, disconnect it.

The number worth tracking is not the pressure but how often you do this. Once a year after bleeding radiators is unremarkable. Once a month is a leak. Once a week is a leak that is getting worse, and repeatedly refilling a leaking system pushes fresh oxygenated water through it, which accelerates corrosion inside the radiators.

That frequency is the single most useful thing you can tell an engineer. It narrows the diagnosis more than the pressure reading itself does.

Pressure too high

Above roughly 2.5 to 3 bar, the pressure relief valve will usually open and discharge outside. Common causes are a filling loop left slightly open, over-filling after a top-up, or an expansion vessel that has lost its charge.

If a valve has discharged, it sometimes fails to reseat afterwards and then weeps continuously — which shows up as the pressure dropping again. The two faults chase each other.

When to call someone

Call if you are refilling more than two or three times a year, if the pressure moves while the heating is off, if there is water dripping from a pipe outside, or if the gauge sits at zero and does not respond to the filling loop at all.

The short version

  • Cold, around 1 to 1.5 bar on most systems — check your manual
  • Higher when hot is normal
  • A sealed system should not lose pressure
  • How often you refill matters more than the reading
